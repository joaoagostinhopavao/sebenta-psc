Chapter 3

Social Networks: Metrics and Folksonomy

Introduction

The previous chapter introduced, alongside the social graph, a second model for organising information in social networks: the interest graph, in which the relevant proximity is no longer between people, but between a person and the topics they interact with (Section 2.2.1). This chapter develops that idea in two complementary directions. First, it presents Social Network Analysis: its purpose, the method that turns a set of relationships between people into a sociogram, and the concrete metrics that answer questions such as “who is most influential in this network?”, “who links groups that would otherwise not communicate?” or “how cohesive is this community?”. A complete example, followed from start to finish, shows how each metric is calculated and, above all, how it is interpreted. Then, it explores the other side of the coin, the organisation of content by topic, through folksonomy, the collective classification that today structures practically every social network through hashtags.

3.1 Social Network Analysis: Purpose and Method

Social Network Analysis (SNA) studies the relationships between people, rather than people in isolation. Most social studies describe each individual by their own characteristics: age, education, income, opinions. Network analysis adds a further question: who each person is connected to, and what place they occupy within that set of relationships (Wasserman and Faust 1994).

This question matters because a person’s position in the network has practical consequences. Two students of the same age, on the same degree and with the same marks can have very different experiences: one knows classmates from several groups and is among the first to hear about an internship or a grant; the other only relates to a small group and rarely receives new information. None of the individual characteristics explains this difference; what explains it is the structure of relationships.

Network analysis began with Moreno’s sociograms (Moreno 1934), drawn by hand to study small groups, such as school classes. Digital platforms changed the scale: every connection, follower or message is recorded automatically, which makes it possible today to analyse networks of millions of people.

3.1.1 What it is for

The questions network analysis answers arise in very different contexts:

  • Education: identifying students nobody chooses to work with, understanding whether a class is fragmented into closed groups, forming more balanced work teams.
  • Organisations: discovering who, in practice, moves information between departments, regardless of the formal organisation chart, and what knowledge is lost if that person leaves.
  • Public health: predicting where a contagious disease will spread fastest and who should be vaccinated or informed first.
  • Digital platforms: suggesting “people you may know”, identifying influential accounts, detecting coordinated networks of fake accounts that artificially amplify a message.

In all these cases, the answer does not lie in people’s attributes, but in the way they are connected.

3.1.2 The method in five steps

A social network analysis generally follows five steps:

  1. Define the question and the boundary of the network. Which relationship is of interest (friendship, collaboration, message exchange, co-authorship) and who belongs to the network (a class, a department, the followers of an account). The same class produces very different networks if the question is “who do you have lunch with?” or “who do you ask for help when studying?”.
  2. Collect the data. This can be done through a sociometric questionnaire, Moreno’s original method, in which each person states who they relate to; through observation; or from digital records (who follows whom, who replies to whom, who wrote with whom).
  3. Represent the network. The data is organised into an adjacency matrix and drawn as a sociogram, the graph that makes the structure visible.
  4. Calculate the metrics. In small networks, the calculations are done by hand; in larger networks, tools such as Gephi or the NetworkX library for the Python language are used.
  5. Interpret. Metrics describe positions, not motives: a value only becomes meaningful when read in the light of the initial question and the context of the group.

To make each step concrete, the rest of this section follows a single example. In a class of nine students, identified from A to I, each student answered the question “Which two classmates would you like to do the next group assignment with?”. There are, therefore, 18 choices in total, two per student.

Sociometric data describes personal relationships and is, by nature, sensitive: knowing that a student was chosen by nobody is information that can hurt or stigmatise. The results should therefore be anonymised, as in this example, and used only for the purpose for which they were collected.

3.2 Representing a Social Network as a Graph

Analysing a social network systematically requires, first of all, representing it as a graph: a set of nodes, representing people or organisations, connected by edges, representing their relationships. This is the same representational logic used by Moreno’s sociogram (Section 1.2.1) and by the social and interest graphs discussed in the previous chapter.

An edge, that is, a connection between two nodes, can be undirected, when it represents a mutual, symmetric relationship (for example, a friendship on Facebook, where both parties have to accept the connection), or directed, when it represents a relationship that only holds in one direction (for example, following someone on Instagram or X, which does not imply being followed back). Two people may also have no relationship at all. This distinction matters: most traditional friendship networks (Facebook, LinkedIn) use undirected connections, while most content-centred networks (Instagram, X, YouTube) use directed connections, which has direct implications for the metrics that follow (Figure 3.1).

Edges can also have a weight, which expresses the intensity of the relationship: the number of messages exchanged between two people, the number of times they interacted, or the number of works they wrote together. In a co-authorship network, for example, two authors who signed three chapters together have a connection of weight 3, stronger than that of two authors who collaborated only once. When the weight is not of interest, every edge counts as 1.

3.2.1 From adjacency matrix to sociogram

The simplest way to record the relationships in a network is the adjacency matrix: a table with one row and one column per person, in which the cell in row i and column j is 1 if i chose j, and is left empty otherwise. In the example class, the rows show who chooses and the columns who is chosen (Table 3.1).

The matrix already contains all the information about the network, but it is hard to read. The sociogram draws exactly the same data as a graph, and the structure becomes obvious: there are two distinct groups, connected only through student E, and there is one student, I, who chose classmates but was chosen by nobody (Figure 3.2).

In networks of a few dozen people, visual reading of the sociogram still works. In networks with thousands or millions of nodes, such as those of digital platforms, the drawing becomes an unreadable blur, and only metrics can describe the structure.

3.3 Metrics for Social Network Analysis

Network analysis metrics fall into two groups: those that describe the position of each node (who is popular, who is central, who acts as a bridge) and those that describe the network as a whole (how connected, how cohesive, how fragmented). In the definitions that follow, n represents the number of nodes in the network; in the example class, n = 9. Unless otherwise stated, position metrics are calculated on each student’s contacts, regardless of the direction of the choice: A and D are connected whether it was A who chose D or the other way round.

3.3.1 Popularity: in-degree and out-degree

The degree of a node is the number of connections it has. In directed graphs, it is useful to distinguish between connections arriving at a node and connections leaving it. In-degree is the number of connections arriving at a node, and is often used as a direct measure of popularity: on Instagram or X, it corresponds, essentially, to the number of followers. Out-degree is the number of connections leaving a node, corresponding, in that same example, to the number of accounts that user follows, and measures each person’s expansiveness, their tendency to reach out to others.

In the class, in-degree is the last row of the matrix: A and C are the most chosen (4 choices each), followed by F (3); I was chosen by nobody. Out-degree, on the other hand, says nothing in this example: it is 2 for everyone, because the question itself limited each student to two choices. This is a first lesson in method: the way data is collected determines what the metrics are able to reveal.

3.3.2 Node density

The density of a node measures the proportion between the number of relationships that node actually has and the number it could have, that is, its degree divided by n − 1 (all the other nodes in the network). If a node has only a third of the connections it could have, given the size of the network, its density is approximately 0.33 (Figure 3.3).

In the class, A has contact with four classmates (B, C, D and I) out of a possible eight, a density of 4/8 = 0.50; I has contact with two (A and D), a density of 2/8 = 0.25. The density of a node should not be confused with the density of the network, a global metric presented further on (Section 3.3.6).

3.3.3 Centrality as Social Power

Centrality measures how well positioned a node is within the network, which is why it is also called social power. There is no single way to measure centrality: sociologist Linton Freeman proposed, in a work now considered foundational to the field, a conceptual clarification that distinguishes at least three distinct notions of centrality (Freeman 1978):

  • Degree centrality: how many direct connections a node has. A node with many connections is, on the face of it, more central.
  • Betweenness centrality: how often a node lies on the shortest path between two other nodes. A node with high betweenness centrality functions as a bridge or broker of information between parts of the network that would otherwise be more distant.
  • Closeness centrality: how close, on average, a node is to all other nodes in the network. A node with high closeness centrality can reach the rest of the network quickly.

A fourth notion responds to a different intuition: what matters is not only how many contacts one has, but who those contacts are.

  • Eigenvector centrality: a node is more central the more central the nodes it is connected to (Bonacich 1987). Being connected to two very well-connected people can be worth more than being connected to five isolated people. PageRank, the algorithm Google originally used to rank web pages, rests on the same principle: a page is important if it is linked to by other important pages (Brin and Page 1998).

These four notions capture different aspects of “importance” in a network, and a node can be central by one measure and peripheral by another: a good example is an intermediary who links two distinct communities, but who, within each of them, has few direct connections, low degree centrality, but very high betweenness centrality (Figure 3.4).

Centrality values are usually presented on a scale from 0 to 1, which makes it possible to compare nodes with each other. In the class, each measure points to a different student:

  • in degree, A and C are tied, with four contacts each;
  • in betweenness, C leads (0.59), but is closely followed by E (0.54), who has only two contacts: E lies on the only path between the two groups, so any information travelling from one group to the other has to pass through them;
  • in closeness, C leads again (0.57), being only a few steps away from everyone;
  • in eigenvector, A comes first (0.53), because all of A’s contacts (B, C, D, I) are in the denser group, whereas C “spends” one of their connections on E, who has few contacts.

3.3.4 Clustering coefficient

The clustering coefficient of a node measures the proportion of pairs of its contacts that are also connected to each other (Watts and Strogatz 1998). It translates the question “are my friends’ friends also my friends?”. A value of 1 indicates that the node belongs to a closed group, in which everyone knows everyone else; a value of 0 indicates that its contacts do not know each other, which is typical of someone who bridges different worlds.

In the class, B has a coefficient of 1: their two contacts, A and C, are connected to each other. E has a coefficient of 0: their contacts, C and F, have no relationship with each other. This is the mechanism platforms exploit when they suggest “people you may know”: if two people have many friends in common, they probably know each other too.

3.3.5 Path and distance

A path between two nodes is any sequence of edges connecting them, directly or indirectly. In a social network, there are typically many possible paths between any two nodes. Distance between two nodes is the length of the shortest path between them, measured by the number of edges traversed. It was precisely this notion of distance that Milgram’s experiment sought to measure empirically (Section 2.1): how many connections, on average, separate two randomly chosen people.

Two global metrics derive from this notion. The diameter is the greatest distance between any two nodes in the network: it indicates how long, in the worst case, a piece of information takes to cross it. The average distance is the mean of the distances between all pairs of nodes. In the class, the diameter is 5 (from I to H, for example, the shortest path is I, A, C, E, F, H) and the average distance is about 2.4 steps.

3.3.6 Network density

Network density measures the proportion between the number of connections that exist in the whole network and the number that would be possible if everyone were connected to everyone. In a directed network with n nodes, each node can choose n − 1 others, so the maximum is n × (n − 1) connections. High-density networks tend to spread information faster, but also to be more susceptible to echo-chamber effects, precisely because almost everyone is connected to almost everyone else.

In the class, there are 18 choices out of a possible 9 × 8 = 72, a density of 18/72 = 0.25. This value, however, requires careful interpretation: since each student could only choose two classmates, 0.25 is the maximum this question allowed. Density is useful for comparing networks collected in the same way (for example, the same class at the start and at the end of the semester), not for comparing networks collected under different rules.

3.3.7 Reciprocity

In directed networks, reciprocity is the proportion of connections that are returned: if A chooses B, does B also choose A? High reciprocity indicates balanced and stable relationships; low reciprocity is typical of popularity networks, such as follower networks, in which many follow a few without being followed back.

In the class, 12 of the 18 choices are reciprocal (A and B, A and C, B and C, E and F, F and G, G and H), a reciprocity of 0.67. The six unreciprocated choices are equally informative: D chose A and C without being chosen by either, and I chose A and D without being chosen by anyone.

3.3.8 Components, cliques and subnetworks

A network may be split into several parts with no connection at all between them, called components. Within a component, more cohesive structures can be identified:

  • a clique is a set of nodes in which everyone is connected to everyone; the smallest is the triangle, with three nodes;
  • a subnetwork or community is a group of nodes with many connections among themselves and few to the outside; identifying them amounts to finding the “groups” in a network.

The class forms a single component: all the students are connected, even if indirectly. It contains four three-member cliques (A-B-C, A-C-D, A-D-I and F-G-H) and splits into two subnetworks: {A, B, C, D, I} and {E, F, G, H}.

3.3.9 Strong ties, weak ties and structural holes

The previous metrics take on a deeper meaning in the light of two classic theories in the sociology of networks.

Sociologist Mark Granovetter showed that, counter-intuitively, new opportunities (a job, an idea, information not yet circulating in the group) come more often through weak ties, acquaintances and occasional contacts, than through strong ties, close friends and family (Granovetter 1973). The reason is structural: close friends almost all know each other and know more or less the same things; it is weak ties that act as a bridge to other circles, with different information. A bridge is a connection whose removal splits the network into disconnected parts.

Ronald Burt called structural holes the empty spaces between groups that do not communicate with each other, and showed that whoever occupies the position spanning them, the broker, has an advantage: they access information from both sides first and control what passes from one to the other (Burt 1992). This is the position that betweenness centrality measures.

In the class, the C-E and E-F connections are the only two bridges in the network, and E is the broker between the two subnetworks. The position has a practical consequence: if E is absent, or changes class, the two groups no longer have any connection at all.

Finally, subnetworks rarely form by chance. Homophily is people’s tendency to connect with others similar to themselves, in age, interests, opinions or background (McPherson, Smith-Lovin, and Cook 2001). It helps explain why closed groups form and, on digital platforms, why recommendation algorithms, by suggesting content and contacts similar to those one already has, tend to reinforce echo chambers.

3.4 Reading a Sociogram in Full

Bringing together the position metrics of every student in a single table completes the picture of the class (Table 3.2).

Read together, the metrics tell a story that none of them tells on its own:

  • A is the most popular student in the larger group and the best connected to classmates who are themselves well connected (highest eigenvector centrality), but hardly acts as a bridge for anyone.
  • C is equally popular and, in addition, holds the most strategic position in the class: closest to everyone and appearing most often on the paths between classmates.
  • E is the most interesting case: chosen only once and with just two contacts, but the broker between the two subnetworks. An analysis based on popularity alone would never have singled them out.
  • I has an in-degree of zero: they chose two classmates and were chosen by neither. They are not isolated, since they have outgoing connections, but their position deserves attention.

Interpretation nonetheless requires caution. Metrics describe positions in a network, not intentions or personal qualities. The question put to the class only collected positive choices: the fact that nobody chose I does not mean I is rejected, only that they are not among anyone’s first preferences. Moreno, precisely for this reason, also asked “who would you not like to work with?” (Moreno 1934). A good analysis always returns to the start of the method: what question was asked, to whom, and what the data does, or does not, allow one to conclude.

3.5 Folksonomy: Collective Content Classification

So far, this chapter has dealt with how to analyse relationships between people. But much of the structure of a modern social network also results from how content is classified and organised, and this is where folksonomy comes in.

The term folksonomy was coined by Thomas Vander Wal in 2007, from the combination of folk (people) and taxonomy (Vander Wal 2007). It denotes the classification of content through a free, collective tagging process, rather than a hierarchy of categories defined a priori by domain experts.

The difference from a traditional taxonomy is structural: in a taxonomy, a group of managers defines a hierarchy of categories in advance, and all content has to fit into it. In a folksonomy, there is no such prior hierarchy: categories, or tags, emerge as users themselves classify content, with no centralised control.

The tagging process can be represented simply: a group of people, or a single person, associates a set of tags with a set of objects (documents, images, posts) (Figure 3.5). Because different people classify the same object in different ways, according to their own perspective, the resulting folksonomy is rich and heterogeneous, but can also contain inconsistencies.

Two types of folksonomy can be distinguished (Vander Wal 2007): broad folksonomy, in which a large number of users tag the same objects, which is useful for identifying the most popular items in a collection, and narrow folksonomy, in which only one or a few people tag each object, typically the content’s own author, which preserves a more personal vocabulary, but makes discovery by others harder.

A common way to visualise the aggregate result of a folksonomy is the tag cloud, a representation in which the most-used tags stand out, typically in larger or darker type, making it possible to quickly identify the dominant themes of a collection (Figure 3.6).

3.5.1 Advantages and problems

Folksonomy has clear advantages: it does not depend on domain experts to define categories, it lets each user classify freely without having to learn a controlled vocabulary, and it captures the real vocabulary of those who actually use the system, not that of those who designed it.

But that same freedom generates well-documented problems: spelling mistakes, morphological variation (for example, “social network” and “social networks” recorded as distinct tags), synonymy (different terms for the same concept), polysemy (the same term with different meanings depending on context), and clutter from low-relevance tags. Current platforms partially mitigate these problems with automatic spell-checking, suggestions of tags already used by others, and semi-automatic grouping of semantically close tags.

3.6 Folksonomy in Practice: from Tags to Hashtags

When the concept of folksonomy became popular, around 2007, the reference examples were systems such as Amazon, YouTube, Flickr and Del.icio.us. It is worth understanding, concretely, how the same idea, free content tagging, produced very different results depending on who was allowed to tag.

On Amazon, until the feature was discontinued in 2013, any customer could attach free tags to a product: the same book could be tagged by different customers as “science fiction”, “dystopia” or “beach read”. Because tagging was open to the entire customer base, a broad folksonomy formed: many voices classifying the same object, which made the most repeated tags a reasonable indicator of collective consensus about what that product actually is.

On YouTube, the mechanism is the opposite, and remains so today: only whoever publishes a video can attach tags to it, through YouTube Studio. Whoever merely watches the video takes no part in its classification. It is, therefore, a narrow folksonomy: it reflects the personal perspective of whoever published the content, not a collective consensus among those who consume it.

Del.icio.us too, a social bookmarking service that let people organise and share web sites through tags, no longer exists: after changing owners several times, it stopped accepting new bookmarks in 2017, when it was acquired by competitor Pinboard, which turned it into a read-only archive.

The mechanism, however, survived the change of platforms, just under another name: the hashtag. By attaching a tag preceded by “#” to a post on Instagram, TikTok or X, each user is taking part in exactly the same collective tagging process described by Vander Wal almost two decades before these platforms came to dominate the market. A study on Instagram hashtags shows precisely that: the folksonomy generated by users through hashtags can be modelled as a complex network, linking posts and profiles that share the same themes, with no central coordination whatsoever among those who use them (Ibba et al. 2015).

The hashtag also inherits the same tensions as classic folksonomy: on Instagram, a popular hashtag like #food has a broad folksonomy, with millions of contributors, but little value for finding something specific; a niche hashtag, created by a small community for a specific event or narrow interest, works more like a narrow folksonomy, more precise, but also less discoverable by those who do not already know about it.

3.7 Summary

This chapter developed two complementary tools for understanding the structure of social networks:

  1. Social Network Analysis studies the structure of relationships between people and follows five steps: define the question and the boundary of the network, collect the data, represent it as an adjacency matrix and a sociogram, calculate metrics and interpret them (Wasserman and Faust 1994)
  2. A social network can be represented as a graph, with directed or undirected edges, weighted or unweighted, depending on the type of relationship
  3. The position metrics of a node are in-degree (popularity) and out-degree, node density, degree, betweenness, closeness (Freeman 1978) and eigenvector centrality (Bonacich 1987), and the clustering coefficient
  4. The global metrics of the network are average distance and diameter, network density, reciprocity, and the division into components, cliques and subnetworks
  5. Weak ties and the brokers who span structural holes link groups that would otherwise not communicate (Granovetter 1973; Burt 1992)
  6. Metrics describe positions, not intentions: their interpretation always depends on the question that produced the data
  7. Folksonomy is the collective classification of content through free tagging, distinct from a hierarchical taxonomy defined a priori (Vander Wal 2007)
  8. Broad and narrow folksonomy represent a trade-off between reach and precision of classification
  9. The hashtag is the contemporary form of folksonomy, present in practically every current social network (Ibba et al. 2015)

3.8 Food for Thought

Think of a social network you use often: among the people you follow, can you identify someone with high betweenness centrality, that is, someone who links groups of people who would not otherwise be in contact? If you drew the sociogram of your class from the question “who do you ask for help when studying?”, which students would you expect to find with high in-degree, and which with high betweenness? Would they be the same?

And what about the hashtags you use or follow: are they broad folksonomy, a generic tag used by millions of people, or narrow folksonomy, specific to a small community? What problems of synonymy or morphological variation have you already noticed in hashtags (for example, two different spellings of the same topic splitting the same conversation into two)?
